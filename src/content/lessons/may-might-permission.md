<div class="lesson-context">
  <p class="lesson-context-lead"><strong>May and Might for Permission and Possibility</strong> are modal verbs that help you express both permission and possibility. Mastering their correct use can enhance clarity and formality in your communication. This lesson focuses on using these modals effectively to ask for, grant permission, and express possibilities.</p>
</div>

## Examples
<div class="lesson-example-grid">
<article class="lesson-example-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Example 1</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Incorrect</span>You might to go to the party tonight.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>You might go to the party tonight.</p>
  <p class="lesson-card-note">'Might' should be followed directly by the base form of the verb without 'to'.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-example-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Example 2</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Incorrect</span>She may to leave early today.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>She may leave early today.</p>
  <p class="lesson-card-note">'May' is used to grant permission and should be followed by the base verb.</p>
</article>
</div>

## How It Works
<div class="lesson-teach-grid">
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-core">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Meaning</p>
    <p>'May' and 'might' are modal verbs used to express permission and possibility. They help communicate whether something is allowed or likely to happen. The formation pattern is: <strong>subject + may/might + base verb</strong>. Understanding these modals is crucial at the B2 level for effective communication.</p>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-when">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Use it when</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Requesting or granting permission in formal contexts.</li>
      <li>Expressing a possibility or uncertainty about future events.</li>
      <li>Writing academic or professional emails where politeness is required.</li>
      <li>Discussing hypothetical situations where outcomes are not certain.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-pattern">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">See it</p>
    <div class="lesson-pattern-stack">
      <div class="lesson-pattern-sentence">You <strong>may</strong> leave early if you finish your work.</div>
      <div class="lesson-pattern-sentence">She <strong>might</strong> attend the meeting if she is available.</div>
    </div>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-remember">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Quick rules</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Use 'may' for formal permission and 'might' for less certain possibilities.</li>
      <li>Always follow 'may' and 'might' with the base form of the verb.</li>
      <li>Do not use 'to' after 'may' or 'might'.</li>
      <li>Use 'might' for hypothetical or less likely events.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
</div>

## Common Mistakes
<div class="lesson-error-grid">
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Common problem 1</p>
  <h3>Using 'to' after 'may' or 'might'</h3>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Weak</span>You may to start the project now.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Strong</span>You may start the project now.</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Never use 'to' after 'may' or 'might'; use the base verb directly.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Common problem 2</p>
  <h3>Confusing 'may' with 'might' for certainty</h3>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Weak</span>He may come to the party, but he is not sure.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Strong</span>He might come to the party, but he is not sure.</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Use 'might' for less certain possibilities.</p>
</article>
</div>

## Why It Matters
<p class="lesson-importance"><strong>&#127919; Why it matters:</strong> Using 'may' and 'might' correctly can significantly affect the tone and formality of your communication. Mastering these modals ensures that your requests and permissions are conveyed with the appropriate level of politeness and clarity, which is crucial in both professional and academic settings. They also help you express possibilities with the right degree of certainty.</p>
