<div class="lesson-context"><p class="lesson-context-lead"><strong>Advanced Ways to Compare</strong> helps you compare people, things, and ideas clearly and effectively. In this lesson, you will learn how to use advanced comparative structures to express nuanced differences and similarities without mixing patterns.</p></div>

## Examples
<div class="lesson-example-grid">
<article class="lesson-example-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Example 1</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Incorrect</span>This project is more better than the last one.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>This project is better than the last one.</p>
  <p class="lesson-card-note">Avoid using double comparatives like 'more better'.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-example-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Example 2</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Incorrect</span>She is the most fastest runner in the team.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>She is the fastest runner in the team.</p>
  <p class="lesson-card-note">Superlatives do not need 'most' if the adjective already ends in '-est'.</p>
</article>
</div>

## How It Works
<div class="lesson-teach-grid">
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-core">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Meaning</p>
    <p>Advanced comparative structures allow you to express nuanced differences and similarities between people, things, or ideas. These structures include phrases like <strong>far more</strong>, <strong>much less</strong>, and <strong>not nearly as ... as</strong>. At the B2 level, mastering these structures enhances your ability to make precise and sophisticated comparisons in both spoken and written English.</p>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-when">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Use it when</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Emphasizing a significant difference between two items.</li>
      <li>Expressing a subtle or minor difference in quality or quantity.</li>
      <li>Comparing abstract ideas or concepts in academic writing.</li>
      <li>Describing trends or changes over time in reports or presentations.</li>
      <li>Engaging in debates or discussions that require nuanced language.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-pattern">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">See it</p>
    <div class="lesson-pattern-stack">
      <div class="lesson-pattern-sentence">This solution is <strong>far more effective</strong> than the previous one.</div>
      <div class="lesson-pattern-sentence">The new policy is <strong>not nearly as comprehensive as</strong> the old one.</div>
      <div class="lesson-pattern-sentence">He is <strong>much less experienced</strong> than his colleagues.</div>
    </div>
  </section>
  <section class="lesson-panel lesson-panel-remember">
    <p class="lesson-panel-label">Quick rules</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Use <strong>far more</strong> or <strong>much less</strong> to emphasize differences.</li>
      <li>Avoid redundancy by not combining <strong>more</strong> with <strong>-er</strong> or <strong>most</strong> with <strong>-est</strong>.</li>
      <li>Use <strong>not nearly as ... as</strong> to highlight a lack of equality.</li>
      <li>Choose the appropriate structure based on the degree of difference you want to express.</li>
      <li>Ensure clarity by matching the comparative structure to the context.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>
</div>

## Common Mistakes
<div class="lesson-error-grid">
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Common problem 1</p>
  <h3>Using double comparatives</h3>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Weak</span>This route is more faster during rush hour.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Strong</span>This route is faster during rush hour.</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Use only one comparative form, either '-er' or 'more'.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Common problem 2</p>
  <h3>Incorrect use of comparatives and superlatives</h3>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Weak</span>It is the cheapest than the old model.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Strong</span>It is cheaper than the old model.</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Use 'than' with comparatives and 'in/of' with superlatives.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Common problem 3</p>
  <h3>Confusing equality with superiority</h3>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Weak</span>He is as more talented as his brother.</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Strong</span>He is as talented as his brother.</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Use 'as ... as' for equality, not with 'more'.</p>
</article>
</div>
