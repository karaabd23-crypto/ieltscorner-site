<div class="lesson-context"><p class="lesson-context-lead">Several CELPIP Listening questions ask about the speaker's intention, attitude, or tone. The answer is almost never in the words alone -- it is in the stress pattern, the contrast signal, or the qualifying phrase. This lesson teaches you to hear intention, not just content.</p></div>

## How It Works
<div class="lesson-teach-grid">
<div class="lesson-teach-panel">
  <p class="lesson-teach-pattern">Hedge phrases = doubt or reluctance</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-meaning">Words like "I suppose", "I guess", "more or less", "in a way" signal the speaker does not fully mean what they say</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-example">"The plan is workable, I suppose." → Intention: mild reluctance, not full endorsement.</p>
</div>
<div class="lesson-teach-panel">
  <p class="lesson-teach-pattern">Contrast signal = main intention follows</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-meaning">"But", "however", "although", "that said" signal that the main point comes AFTER the connector</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-example">"The location is convenient. However, the cost is prohibitive." → Main intention: concerns about cost.</p>
</div>
<div class="lesson-teach-panel">
  <p class="lesson-teach-pattern">Emphasis word = key point</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-meaning">A stressed word or "the real issue is", "what matters is" signals the speaker's focus</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-example">"The REAL question is whether we can deliver on time." → Intention: focus on delivery timeline.</p>
</div>
<div class="lesson-teach-panel">
  <p class="lesson-teach-pattern">Rhetorical question = criticism or scepticism</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-meaning">A question the speaker answers themselves is often used to express doubt or disagreement</p>
  <p class="lesson-teach-example">"Can we really afford that? I don't think so." → Intention: opposition.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Common Mistakes
<div class="lesson-error-grid">
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Taking words at face value</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Avoid</span>"The speaker said it was a good idea, so their intention is positive support."</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>"The speaker said 'it might be a good idea' with a hedge -- their intention is uncertain or conditional support."</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> In natural speech, "might be good" is significantly weaker than "is good". The modal and the stress carry intention.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Missing the contrast pivot</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Avoid</span>"The speaker praised the location, so the intention is to support the proposal."</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>"The speaker praised the location but then focused on the cost problem. The main intention is to raise a concern."</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Look for the contrast word ("but", "however") to find where the speaker's real point is.</p>
</article>
<article class="lesson-error-card">
  <p class="lesson-card-label">Ignoring tone for content</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-weak"><span>Avoid</span>"The speaker gave three advantages, so the intention must be to recommend."</p>
  <p class="lesson-line lesson-line-strong"><span>Better</span>"The speaker gave three advantages and then said 'but I'm not convinced' -- the intention is to express scepticism despite recognising the advantages."</p>
  <p class="lesson-fix-line"><strong>Fix:</strong> Intention questions are rarely answered by content alone. The tone modifier at the end of the turn carries the actual intention.</p>
</article>
</div>

## Why It Matters
<p class="lesson-importance">CELPIP Listening intention questions are designed to test whether you hear what speakers mean, not just what they say. Native English speech is full of pragmatic signals (hedges, pivots, emphases) that modify the literal meaning of words. Candidates who listen only for content miss these signals and consistently choose the wrong intention option. Training yourself to hear hedge phrases, contrast pivots, and emphasis markers converts the most subjective question type into a pattern-recognition task.</p>
